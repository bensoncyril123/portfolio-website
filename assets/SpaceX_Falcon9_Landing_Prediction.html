Why This Matters

Problem Statement

SpaceX advertises Falcon 9 launches at a fraction of the competition's price, and that pricing gap traces back almost entirely to a single engineering capability: reusability.

The Booster Being Reused

Falcon 9 has gone through several first-stage revisions — v1.0, v1.1, the upgraded v1.2 "Full Thrust," and the current Block 5 — alongside the triple-booster Falcon Heavy derivative. Across every variant, the first stage is the rocket's most expensive section: nine Merlin engines, the bulk of the airframe, and the avionics and landing legs needed to fly it back down intact. That's the part SpaceX recovers and re-flies; the second stage, which carries the payload the rest of the way to orbit, is expended on every mission regardless of outcome.

Manufacturing a first stage from scratch is the dominant cost in building a Falcon 9. Recovering and refurbishing one instead — inspecting the engines, swapping a handful of components, requalifying it for flight — costs a fraction of that. Whether a given booster lands successfully is therefore not just an engineering outcome; it's the single biggest lever on what that launch actually costs SpaceX to fly.

Key insight — If we can predict, ahead of launch, whether the first stage will land — using only information available before liftoff, such as payload mass, target orbit, launch site, and the booster's own flight history — we have a usable proxy for what that launch costs SpaceX to fly. That's exactly the intelligence a competing launch provider would want before bidding against SpaceX for a contract. Everything from here forward is about collecting that pre-launch information, getting it into a clean, modelable form, and turning it into a working classifier.
Stage 1

Data Collection

Every Falcon 9 launch record used here comes from two independently maintained sources, reconciled against each other rather than taken on faith from either one alone.

SpaceX REST API

Structured launch & core data

Rocket configuration, payload, launch site, and booster core/landing data pulled directly from SpaceX's own public API — the closest thing to a primary source available here, since it's the same data SpaceX's own systems track internally.

Web Scraping

Wikipedia launch tables

The "List of Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches" Wikipedia page, maintained by an active community of spaceflight watchers, was parsed with BeautifulSoup into a second, independently sourced record of the same flights.

Key insight — Treating the API and Wikipedia as two independent witnesses to the same events — rather than picking one and trusting it outright — let every field get cross-checked against a second copy before it touched a model. A booster serial number or landing outcome that disagreed between sources surfaced immediately as something to investigate, instead of silently corrupting downstream features the way an unverified single source could.
Stage 2

Data Wrangling

The raw Outcome field packs two pieces of information into one string — where the booster attempted to land, and whether it actually stuck the landing (e.g. "True ASDS", "False Ocean", "None None") — and had to be decomposed into a clean binary target before any model could learn from it.

Engineering the landing label

Each of the eight raw outcome strings was mapped to Class = 1 (booster landed intact) or Class = 0 (it didn't), collapsing the location qualifier — ocean, RTLS ground pad, or ASDS drone ship — and the boolean success flag into the single target the model actually needs. A follow-up pass showed LandingPad missing for 40.6% of records. Rather than treat that as a data-quality problem to impute away, the missingness itself turned out to be informative: it marks exactly the flights where no landing was ever attempted in the first place.

Key insight — "No landing attempted" flights share the exact same missing-data signature as outright landing failures — both leave LandingPad blank. That's a textbook case of data missing not at random, and it mattered for modeling: folding both cases into Class = 0, rather than dropping the rows or treating a blank pad as an error, keeps the 66.7% success rate honest. Drop those rows instead, and both the apparent success rate and any model trained on it would have come out quietly optimistic.
Stage 3a

Exploratory Data Analysis — SQL

Before a single chart got drawn, the launch records were loaded into a Db2 table and interrogated directly in SQL — a deliberately low-tech sanity check that catches schema and aggregation errors before they have a chance to hide inside a plot.

Key Findings from SQL

A handful of direct queries against the Db2 launch table established the basics before any plotting began. The raw records resolve to four distinct launch-site strings — really three physical pads, since Cape Canaveral appears under two names alongside Kennedy and Vandenberg. Payload figures tell their own story: NASA's CRS resupply missions alone account for 45,596 kg of cargo carried to orbit, while the early Falcon 9 v1.1 booster averaged a comparatively modest 2,928 kg per flight — a sign of how much capacity grew as the vehicle matured, with the Falcon 9 Block 5 family eventually carrying the heaviest payloads on record. On the landing side, the first successful ground-pad touchdown came on December 22, 2015 — years after drone-ship recovery attempts had already begun. Between 2010 and 2017, those ocean-platform landings split evenly at five successes and five failures, with ten further flights never attempting a landing at all.

Stage 3b

Exploratory Data Analysis — Visualization

Before any algorithm sees this data, it's worth looking at it directly. Plotting flight number, payload mass, launch site, and orbit against landing outcome (● failure, ● success) surfaces the patterns a classifier will later have to learn — and flags which variables are even worth feeding it.

Orbit Types in This Dataset

Every mission targets a specific orbit, and — as the charts below show — orbit type turns out to be one of the strongest predictors of landing success. Ten categories appear in the data: LEO and VLEO (the closest, under 2,000 km and 450 km), ISS (the station's own low-Earth path), MEO (mid-altitude, home to GPS satellites), the geostationary pair GTO and GEO (~35,786 km, matching Earth's rotation), SSO (fixed local solar time on every pass), PO (over both poles each revolution), HEO (a high-eccentricity ellipse), and ES-L1 (the gravitational balance point between Earth and the Sun).

Flight Number vs. Payload Mass

Each of the 90 flights plotted by its position in the launch sequence — a rough proxy for elapsed time and accumulated engineering experience — against the payload mass it carried.

Key insight — Failures show up throughout the program rather than clustering only in the earliest flights, but success becomes the overwhelming norm after roughly flight #60 — regardless of how heavy the payload is. That's the first hint that flight number is acting as a stand-in for something else entirely: how mature the booster design and recovery procedure had become by the time of launch.

Flight Number vs. Launch Site

Which launch pad flew which flights over time, and how each site's track record evolved as the program matured.

Key insight — CCAFS SLC-40 flew the earliest missions in the program — and carries nearly all of the early failures that came with them — while KSC LC-39A and VAFB SLC-4E only came online roughly a third of the way through the flight history and show an overwhelmingly successful record since. Site and "experience era" are tangled together here, which is worth remembering before reading too much into launch site on its own.

Payload Mass vs. Launch Site

The full payload range each pad has handled, colored by landing outcome.

Key insight — VAFB SLC-4E only ever lofts lighter, polar-orbit payloads and shows a clean record doing it. CCAFS SLC-40 covers the widest payload range of any site and accounts for most of the dataset's failures, concentrated below about 7,000 kg — though that's partly circumstantial: it's also the pad that flew the most missions overall, including nearly all of the earliest, least-proven ones.

Landing Success Rate by Orbit

Mean landing-success rate grouped by target orbit — effectively a groupby().mean() on the Class column.

Key insight — ES-L1, GEO, HEO and SSO missions landed successfully 100% of the time, while GTO — the program's highest-energy orbit — had the lowest success rate at just 50%. Worth flagging as a data scientist, not just a fan: three of those four "perfect" orbits rest on a single flight each, so 100% there means "didn't fail yet," not "guaranteed." GTO's 50%, built on 27 flights, is the only orbit-level success rate in this dataset large enough to take at face value.

Flight Number vs. Orbit

When in the program's history each orbit type was flown, colored by outcome.

Key insight — VLEO missions — flown almost exclusively in the back half of the program — are nearly all successful, while GTO missions are spread across nearly the entire flight history and show the most evenly mixed outcomes of any orbit, consistent with its 50% success rate over a meaningful sample of 27 flights.

Payload Mass vs. Orbit

Payload mass plotted against target orbit, colored by outcome.

Key insight — The heaviest payloads in the entire dataset (roughly 13,000–15,500 kg) all go to VLEO and land successfully every time, while GTO — clustered around 5,000–7,000 kg — has the most evenly mixed success/failure split of any orbit. Payload mass and orbit move together here, which is exactly the kind of correlated-feature pattern a model needs more than one variable to untangle.

Landing Success Rate Trend by Year

Yearly average success rate, 2010–2020, with the shaded band showing the uncertainty around each year's estimate.

Key insight — Success climbed from 0% in 2010–2013 to above 90% by 2019, with a temporary dip in 2018 worth noting rather than explaining away — the early years are built from only a handful of flights each, so single-year wobbles are expected. The broader shape is unambiguous, though: landing was a capability SpaceX learned and improved over the life of the program, not a fixed property of the booster design.
Stage 4

Interactive Visual Analytics — Geography

Zooming out from individual flights to the launch sites themselves shows that their locations were never arbitrary — they're dictated by orbital mechanics and range safety as much as by real estate.

Launch Site Geography

An interactive Folium map of all three launch complexes, with marker clusters colored green for successful and red for failed landings at each site. Pan and zoom to explore individual launches — the underlying coordinates come from the same dataset used everywhere else in this report.

Key insight — All of SpaceX's pads sit within about a kilometer of open water — the nearest coastline point measured just 0.87 km from the launch pad — so a failed ascent falls into the ocean rather than a populated area. They also sit as close to the equator as the continental US allows: Earth's own rotational speed adds free velocity to an eastward launch, and that bonus is largest near the equator and shrinks toward the poles. Each site still keeps direct rail and highway access for hauling boosters in, while staying buffered from nearby cities.

Site Locations at a Glance

A static latitude/longitude view of the same pads — Cape Canaveral, FL and Vandenberg, CA — for a quick geographic reference.

Key insight — The two Florida complexes sit only a few kilometers apart on the same coast, while Vandenberg's polar-orbit pad sits roughly 3,700 km away on the opposite coast — far enough that a southward polar launch from Florida would otherwise overfly populated land, which is exactly the kind of constraint that decides where a launch site can go.
Stage 4

Interactive Dashboard

A Plotly Dash app built on top of the same dataset lets a user filter interactively by launch site and payload range; these are snapshots of its default, all-sites view, included here because an interactive filter is easier to feel than to describe in static prose.

60 of the 90 launches (66.7%) ended in a successful first-stage landing — the same figure confirmed three separate times now, via SQL, pandas, and this dashboard. That 66.7% is also the bar any model has to clear: a classifier that did nothing but always guess "success" would already be right two-thirds of the time.

KSC LC-39A (77%) and VAFB SLC-4E (77%) both out-perform CCAFS SLC-40 (60%) on success rate, even though CCAFS SLC-40 handled by far the most launches — 55 of the 90 — largely because it's also the pad that's been flying the longest, early failures included.

Failures concentrate at lower-to-mid payload masses across CCAFS SLC-40 and KSC LC-39A, reinforcing that payload mass alone doesn't drive failure — it's the combination of site and orbit that matters most.

The same orbit-success pattern from the static EDA holds up here: ES-L1/GEO/HEO/SSO at 100% (each resting on a thin sample, as noted earlier), GTO lowest at 50% on the program's largest single orbit cohort.

Stage 5

Predictive Analysis — Classification

Four classification algorithms — Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors — were each tuned with GridSearchCV (10-fold cross-validation) over an 80/20 train/test split, then scored on the held-out test set the tuning process never touched. With only 90 flights to work with, that train/test discipline matters more than usual — it's the only thing standing between a genuinely predictive model and one that has simply memorized the training data.

Model Comparison

Recall the baseline from the dashboard: a model that always predicts "success" is right 66.7% of the time without learning anything. Every algorithm here clears that bar — the real question is by how much, and how reliably.

Winning Model: Decision Tree

Confusion matrix on the 18-flight held-out test set — the 20% of flights the model never saw while it was being tuned.

Key insight — The tuned decision tree correctly identified all 12 actual successful landings (zero false negatives) and correctly flagged 5 of the 6 actual failures — misclassifying only one failed landing as a success, for 94.4% overall accuracy. For this project's use case, that's the error worth watching: a false "it will land" is the one that would lead a competitor to underestimate SpaceX's cost advantage, while a false "it won't land" only makes them overly cautious.

Conclusion

Landing success is predictable — and learnable

A tuned Decision Tree classifier predicts whether a Falcon 9 first stage will land successfully with 94.4% accuracy on unseen flights, using only information known before launch — payload mass, target orbit, launch site, and booster flight history. Across both the exploratory analysis and the model itself, orbit type and launch site emerge as stronger predictors of landing outcome than payload mass alone, and the year-over-year trend confirms that landing success was a capability SpaceX learned and improved over the life of the program rather than a fixed property of the rocket.

It's worth being precise about what this result can and can't claim. Ninety flights is a small dataset by machine-learning standards, the 94.4% figure rests on an 18-flight test set, and the program itself was still evolving its hardware year over year — which means some of what looks like a "launch site effect" or "orbit effect" is partly a "when in the program's history" effect wearing a disguise. None of that erases the signal in the data; it means the right way to use a model like this is as a continuously retrained estimate that improves with every new flight, not a one-time verdict.

Key insight — Because a successful landing is what lets SpaceX reuse a booster — and reuse is what drives its launch cost down — this kind of pre-launch prediction is exactly the signal a competing launch provider would want when sizing up how aggressively SpaceX can price a given contract.
